A concept paper for IT teams  ·  marked-up edition, October 2026

The agent that already knows your estate

Every IT team knows more than it has written down, and the parts it has written down drift out of date in ways nobody notices until they cost someone an afternoon. General AI assistance does not help much with this: it is fluent about technology in general and ignorant of yours.

This paper sets out one way to close that gap. It describes a concept — an assistant that carries your organisation's own documented knowledge and your team's own procedures — and an existing, working tool that implements it, which is open and intended to be adapted rather than adopted as-is. It covers the problem, the shape of the solution, how such a system is built and deployed, and a staged way to introduce it to a team without taking on much risk. The specifics of any one organisation are deliberately left out; the shape is what transfers.

About this edition

The original paper was written at the end of August 2026, when the system had one user: the person who built it. This is the same paper, marked up five weeks after it was put in front of a real team. In that time the harness underneath it moved through four upstream releases and a major version, the team's library of procedures and context was rebuilt around a knowledge base, three technicians received the tool on their working machines, and the first unattended job started running in shadow mode.

Nothing from the original has been removed. Where a claim held, it is stamped as such. Where experience said otherwise, the original is struck through and the correction written beside it. Where we still don't know, the note says so. A new stage, five-b, holds the usage figures, and a closing stage holds the author's own notes, which are opinion and marked as opinion.

Stage one · the problem

Your best documentation lives in three people's heads

Every IT team has the same asymmetry. The senior technician can tell you which switch serves that room, why the printer on the second floor is on a static lease, and which of two identical-looking sites uses the older firmware — instantly, from memory.

None of that is written down in a form anyone else can use quickly. Some of it is written down and wrong. Most of it was true when it was written and has drifted since.

The cost shows up in ordinary moments:

  • A newer technician spends forty minutes establishing something a colleague knows cold.
  • The one person who knows is on leave, at another site, or in a meeting.
  • A ticket gets solved correctly, and the reasoning evaporates the moment it closes.
  • Someone follows the documentation and it is out of date, so next time they don't bother.

That last one matters most. Documentation that has burned you once stops being consulted, which means it stops being corrected, which guarantees it keeps being wrong. Every team has a wiki in this condition.

Field note · October 2026

The opening example was not hypothetical. The single most-used question in five weeks of team use was a variant of “which switch serves that room?” — and the first time a technician asked it, the directory of rooms held an answer for fewer than a third of them. Of 125 people in the staff directory the system started with, 40 had a room recorded. The rest were in exactly the place this stage describes: in someone's head.

Stage two · why not a general assistant

The value isn't the model. It's your context.

A general-purpose assistant is genuinely good at explaining a protocol or drafting a script. It knows nothing about your naming conventions, your site layout, your ticket queues, or the exception someone made three years ago that everything now depends on.

So the useful question is not “can a model help with IT work?” — it plainly can — but “how does a model come to know what our team knows?”

Generic assistant

“How should I troubleshoot an access point that's offline?”

Returns a competent, general procedure. Correct, and no faster than what the technician already knows.

Context-aware agent

“What do we know about the access points at the north site?”

Answers from your documented estate — models, firmware, addressing, the standing exceptions and why they exist.

The second question is the one technicians actually ask each other. It has never had a good answer that wasn't a person.

Field note · what people actually asked

Counted from the procedures the agent loaded across 66 sessions: find past tickets like this one (18 times), what should I be looking at (17), check the network for a room (14), brief me on one ticket (8), what is open (7), is there an article about this (7), what closed (6), who is this person and where do they sit (4), printers (4). Every one of them is a question about this estate and this queue. Nobody asked the agent to explain a protocol.

The value also came from live context, not only written context. The questions people came back for were answered from a ticket cache refreshed every few minutes and a reachability check run at that moment, with the documentation supplying the names and the map. Static documentation alone would have answered the fourth-most-common question, not the first three.

Stage three · what it is

Two things: your context, and your procedures

The implementation this paper draws on is GAH — the Good Agent Harness, an open project that provides the agent itself along with the machinery to deploy it to a team and govern what it may do. In use it is simply a terminal application a technician opens and talks to.

It is worth being precise about what GAH is and is not, because it is only half of what makes the result useful. GAH supplies the vehicle: the agent, the deployment mechanism, and the controls. It ships with no knowledge of your organisation and no opinion about how your team works — deliberately, since that is the part no external project could get right.

The other half is yours, and it is what the rest of this section describes: two bodies of material that you write, keep in your own version control, and improve over time.

Your organisational context

The documented facts of your estate: sites, networks, equipment inventories, conventions, people and their roles. Not pasted into a prompt each time — carried by the agent as a matter of course, so a technician can simply ask.

Field note · the context became data

In August the context was ten Markdown documents. By mid-September most of it had been taken apart into structured data files — a people directory, printers, switches, firewalls, networks, sites, per-room notes — with articles rendered from them, and the agent's lookup tools reading the same files. The reason was practical: a procedure that reads a directory can also write to it, one fact at a time, with an undo. Prose cannot be updated that way by a tool, and “append a sentence to a document” is not a correction anyone trusts a machine to make.

The rule that came out of it, now written into the guidance a new deployment reads before it writes its third procedure: the skill is the procedure; the knowledge base is the facts the procedure needs. A fact embedded in a skill goes stale silently and cannot be fixed by the person who just discovered it was wrong. A fact in the knowledge base can.

Your skills

A skill is a packaged procedure: how your team triages a ticket, drafts a reply, checks the status of a device, writes an article. Each declares what it is for and precisely which tools and data it may touch. Staff invoke them by name or just describe what they want.

Neither is exotic. What makes the combination valuable is that both are yours, both are versioned, and both improve without anyone reinstalling anything.

The tool is the vehicle. What your team knows is the cargo — and no two organisations carry the same load.

Field note · the tool became interchangeable

The skill file format used here became an open standard in December 2025, and by autumn 2026 it is read by most of the assistant products a team might buy as well as by this harness. The procedures a team writes are now portable in a way they were not when this paper was drafted; the knowledge base is plain data behind a small set of lookup tools, which the same products can also reach. The practical consequence for a team deciding what to build: invest in the context and procedures, and treat the tool that runs them as a choice you can revisit.

Stage four · the architecture

An open harness underneath, your policy on top

The design question for anything like this is where the boundary sits between what you build and what you inherit. Build too much and you maintain an agent framework forever. Build too little and you cannot make it safe for your environment.

GAH is itself built on an open agent harness (earendil-works/pi): the terminal interface, tool use, session handling. That harness is vendored into the project rather than merely depended upon. Upstream improvements can be pulled in deliberately, reviewed as a normal change, and pinned. On top of that sits a layer that is entirely yours.

Deployment policy
yours
Per-user configuration held centrally: which models may be used, which skills a given person receives, which credentials are present, and whether a capability is read-only or permitted to change something. Staff cannot self-modify what they have been granted.
Skills & context
yours
A shared set maintained centrally, refreshed on every launch so a correction reaches everyone without anyone acting — plus an optional personal set each technician can extend, which the refresh never overwrites.
Open agent harness
inherited · vendored
Terminal interface, tool execution, conversation and session management. Tracked upstream so improvements arrive, held at a reviewed version so they arrive on your schedule.

The separation is the point. You inherit the parts that are hard and generic, and own the parts that are specific and consequential — access, safety, and what your team is actually asked to do.

Field note · where the maintenance actually went

The separation held. The cost did not land where the paper implied. The “entirely yours” layer stayed small: a handful of policy extensions and a per-person configuration file. The inherited layer is where the hours went. In five weeks the upstream harness moved from version 0.85 to 1.0.4 — a major version and 174 commits in the last jump alone — and GAH re-anchored its nine modifications to the harness four times. Two or three of them had to be rewritten at each large sync because the code underneath had moved.

Two incidents stand out. The scheduled sync that was supposed to surface upstream drift had failed fifteen weeks in a row without anyone noticing, because its only signal was an email; a daily check that files an issue when it breaks replaced it. And one upstream release added a second way to list models that the policy's allowlist did not know about. Re-applied mechanically, the modification would have left every denied model reachable through the new path. It was caught because the sync was done by hand, with tests written for both paths. Vendoring gives control, and the cost is a careful review every time upstream releases.

The same releases shipped capabilities the deployment keeps off: connections to external tool servers, model-written code running in a sandbox, new sign-in flows. None reached the team, because the policy layer refuses everything it has not been told to allow. The separation worked as designed. Even so, pulling in an upstream release means reviewing what arrived, not only checking that it builds.

Safety is a property of the layer you own

Because the policy layer is yours, capabilities can be split rather than granted wholesale. Asking what a device's configuration is and changing that configuration are different permissions, even where they use the same underlying tool. A team can be given the first for months before anyone considers the second.

Equally, actions taken on your systems can be attributed to the person who took them rather than to a shared robot identity — which matters the first time you need to know who changed something.

Field note · attribution, in numbers

Every person has their own identity with the model provider and their own audit log on the server, so the provider's own invocation record says who made each call, and the audit log says which tool ran for whom. Across roughly 430 tool invocations by four people, the policy blocked one: a shell command from a session whose configuration did not permit a shell. Nobody tried to widen their own grant, and nobody needed to. The split between reading and changing held without friction because the procedures were written to it from the start.

Field note · why the numbers went down

The library was rebuilt for a team rather than for its author. Nine skills written for one person's analysis — long-running triage reports, batch summaries — were retired or moved to a schedule, because a technician mid-task will not wait four minutes for an answer. What replaced them was faster and narrower: what should I look at, brief me on this one, who sits in that room, each answering in seconds from a local cache. The original 22 included skills one person wanted; the 13 that remain are the ones the team used. Expect your library to shrink once real users start using it. That is a healthy sign, not a setback.

What a skill actually looks like

A skill is a Markdown file with a short header. The header is the whole security model — everything below it is prose telling the agent how your team does the thing. 

---
name: ticket-triage
description: Analyze open support tickets and provide triage recommendations
allowed-tools: Bash(~/ops/bin/ticket-triage.sh:*), Bash(~/ops/bin/day-of-week.sh:*)
---

# ...instructions, examples, and rules follow in plain Markdown

name and description are how the agent decides the skill is relevant to what someone asked. allowed-tools is the hard boundary: this skill may run those two scripts and nothing else. It cannot read arbitrary files, call other systems, or reach for a tool nobody granted it.

Correction · where the boundary really is

In GAH the hard boundary is the deployment policy, not the skill header: a per-person allowlist of tools, enforced by the harness, plus the fact that every external system sits behind a wrapper script the person's account may or may not run. The header documents the skill's intent and helps the model choose, and a review of the guidance in September found it was being described as enforcement when it is not. The original paragraph above was written from a different tool's semantics. The lesson generalises: put the boundary where it is enforced, and describe it there.

Why the tools are wrapper scripts rather than raw commands

Every external system is reached through a small script rather than by giving the agent direct command access. That keeps credentials and connection details out of the skill definitions entirely — the skill knows a script exists, not how it authenticates — and it collapses permissions to one clear grant per skill instead of a sprawl of patterns.

It also makes the read/write split practical: where a script exposes named operations, a skill can be granted the ones that only read and denied the ones that change something, using the same underlying tool.

Held · and it did more than keep secrets

The wrapper scripts carried the whole team's experience. Of 424 tool calls across five weeks, 314 were wrapper scripts and 108 were the agent reading a skill to learn how to use one. A script that formats a table, ranks a queue, or answers a lookup deterministically gave the same answer to every technician, and improving it improved everyone's next session. The model supplied judgement and wording; the scripts supplied facts. Keep that division.

What the context covers

Ten Markdown documents Twenty articles and eight data files, versioned like code and reviewed like code. Generically, they hold:

  • People and structure — staff and leadership, roles and titles, contact details, which site someone works from, who escalates to whom
  • Sites and networks — locations and their abbreviations, network topology, addressing
  • Equipment inventories — the managed estate, model by model, with the standing exceptions and the reasons for them
  • Working conventions — how the ticket system is actually used, priority definitions, tone and escalation expectations
  • Time-based facts — the recurring annual calendar, historical baselines to compare current activity against

This is the part that makes the difference between a competent general assistant and one that can answer “who owns this, where is it, and what did we decide about it last year?” It is also, deliberately, the least exotic part of the system: plain Markdown data and Markdown that a person can read, correct, and review.

Proven against real work

The library has been exercised against live ticket queues and live infrastructure — triaging real tickets, answering real questions about the estate, and running unattended on a schedule as well as interactively. The skills that survived are the ones that earned their place; several early ones were removed for being clever rather than useful.

The hard part was never the agent. It was writing down what the team already knew, in a form something else could use.

Field note · one regular, two occasional

This split is the main finding. One technician made the tool part of their day within a week and has not stopped. Two others used it when prompted and otherwise did not open it — not from objection, as far as anyone can tell, but because nothing in their day required them to. Their queues are small; the question “what should I work on?” has an obvious answer for them without a tool.

The owner's conclusion, recorded at the time: people open the tool when their day needs it, not because they were told to. The response was to change what opening it feels like — a plan for the day, three tickets with a reason each, printed before the first prompt — rather than to ask people to use it more. Whether that works is not yet known.

Held · updates arrived without anyone acting

The shared procedures were changed 102 times in five weeks, and the knowledge base 51 times. No technician installed anything. Every change reached them at their next launch. Two things had to be added that the paper did not foresee: people left sessions open overnight and were therefore running yesterday's procedures, so the agent now says in-session when updates are waiting; and after a harness upgrade the team was shown several hundred lines of the upstream project's release notes, so the deployment now carries its own short “what's new” for its own users instead.

Stage six · getting it onto machines

Deployment is a push, not a project

Nothing about this requires visiting desks. The agent is delivered the same way you already deliver software to managed machines: a script pushed through your endpoint management platform, or a global policy applied to the relevant group.

A technician sees a shortcut appear. They click it, and they are in. There is no per-machine configuration to get wrong, no runtime for staff to install, and nothing they need to keep up to date — updates arrive when they next open it.

Removal is the same operation in reverse, which is worth saying plainly: piloting this with one person costs one push and can be undone with another.

Held · with three surprises, all in the terminal

The push worked as described: a shortcut, a key, a window. What took real time was everything between the window and the agent. Copy and paste from the session did not work on the team's terminal until the server's session multiplexer was reconfigured; long links wrapped into dead fragments because that multiplexer predates the escape sequence that keeps them whole; and the server, sized for one person, had two gigabytes of memory for a team. None of these were problems with the agent, but they were the first things a new user ran into.

One topology that works

The obvious next question is what actually lands on the endpoint. In the running implementation, the answer is: a shortcut, and nothing else. The agent itself runs in a session on a server the technician connects to.

Endpoint
Staff machine
  • A shortcut that opens a terminal session
No runtime. No credentials. No data at rest.
Server
Where the agent runs
  • The agent session itself
  • Skills and organisational context, checked out and refreshed at launch
  • The wrapper scripts that reach other systems — executed here
  • Credentials, held per user and readable only by that user
Elsewhere
Sources & systems
  • The model provider you choose
  • Version control holding skills and context
  • Ticketing system
  • Infrastructure and management APIs

endpoint → server over an ordinary remote session  ·  server → version control at launch  ·  server → systems via wrapper scripts  ·  server → model provider

Three consequences follow, and they are the reason for the shape:

  • Nothing sensitive is on the laptop. A lost or replaced machine is a hardware event, not a security one. No credential rotation, no data to wipe.
  • One place to update. Skills and context are refreshed when a session starts, so a correction reaches everyone the next time they open it — with no redeployment and nothing for staff to install.
  • Network access lives in one place. The server already has reach into internal systems, so endpoints need no new firewall exceptions or VPN paths just to make the agent useful.
  • The model is reached from your side. The server holds the provider credentials and makes the calls, so staff machines never hold those either — and the traffic leaves from infrastructure you control.

“So which model do we use?”

Whichever you decide. The agent is not tied to a particular model or vendor — the provider is a configuration choice, and a reversible one. Teams pick differently for good reasons: an existing cloud agreement, a procurement preference, where the data is permitted to go, or simply cost.

Two details make that choice manageable rather than merely possible:

  • It is set centrally, per person. Model access is part of the same per-user configuration that governs skills and credentials — not something staff select, and not something they can widen.
  • Models are named explicitly, never by wildcard. A provider adding or renaming a model cannot silently change what your team is using, or quietly move you onto something you have not evaluated or budgeted for.

Because the agent runs on your server, the provider is called from your infrastructure under your account. Changing provider later is a configuration change on one machine — not a redeployment, and nothing staff would notice beyond the answers.

Held · “where the data is permitted to go” stopped being hypothetical

A new model arrived at the provider in late September, available at first only through a routing profile that may process requests in any of the provider's regions, not only the team's own. The deployment's explicit model list meant the question had to be asked rather than silently answered: the model was allowed for a week on that profile, as a conscious decision, and moved to a same-region profile the day one existed, at ten percent more per token. The catalogue the harness could have exposed by wildcard held 177 entries. Eight were allowed. Naming models explicitly is a governance control, not just a configuration setting.

The terminal is not the only front door

The topology settles two things independently: where the agent runs, and how people reach it. Everything above concerns the first. The second is a separate choice, and a terminal session is simply what this implementation happens to use.

A browser interface reaching the same server-side agent would change nothing architecturally — same skills, same context, same credentials, same controls, same single place to update — while removing the one thing that puts some people off before they have tried it. Not every technician is at home on a command line, and for a tool whose entire value depends on people choosing to use it, that is not a small consideration.

This has not been built. A prototype has. It is worth mentioning because it is nearer than it sounds: the underlying harness already supports running as a server that authenticated clients connect to over a defined protocol. A web front end would be another client of that server rather than a second system — and both could exist at once, for the same team, talking to the same agent. Someone who prefers the terminal keeps it; someone who does not, never sees it.

Open · a browser version exists; whether to adopt it is undecided

In October a two-file prototype — one web page, one small relay, no framework — ran a real session through the browser, on a laptop against a local agent and on the shared server over an ordinary encrypted tunnel, with the same policy, audit and model allowlist as the terminal. It worked on both operating systems the team uses. It is still a prototype and deliberately not wired into anything; the unresolved parts are a launcher mode that does not assume a terminal, and a first run that does not need one.

What the exercise clarified is the choice itself: for people who will never want a terminal, the choice is between a browser client of this agent, a terminal-in-a-browser, or a product the organisation already pays for that can read the same skills and reach the same knowledge base. The author's view is in the closing stage.

That is the more general point, and it applies well beyond the interface: the pieces here are deliberately separable. Where it runs, how people reach it, which model answers, and what any given person is permitted to do are four independent decisions, and none of them is load-bearing for the others.

This is one deployment, not a requirement

A server session happens to answer several problems at once, which is why it was chosen — but nothing in the concept depends on it. The agent could run locally on each machine, or in a container per user, and the trade-offs simply move: local execution removes a dependency and a network hop, at the cost of putting credentials and updates back onto endpoints.

The parts worth carrying across any topology are the principles rather than the boxes: keep credentials off endpoints, keep skills and context in version control, and refresh them at launch so nobody is running a stale copy.

Held · the other topology got built too

Within a week of the paper, a second kind of organisation asked for the local topology: managed Windows machines behind an authenticating proxy, with an internal inference gateway as the only permitted model endpoint, and no server to connect to. So a per-machine package now exists — an installer, a shortcut, pinned tools, the same policy layer — and it taught two things the server never would have. The build must not need the network, which it now does not. And some gateways refuse to let a model call tools at all, so the harness learned to carry tool calls as plain text when it must. The principles in the caution note above transferred unchanged; the specific setup did not, as the note anticipated.

Stage seven · the part that compounds

It improves because people use it

This is the difference between a tool that plateaus and one that appreciates, and it turns the documentation problem from Stage One inside out.

When a technician asks the agent about part of the estate and the answer is thin, wrong, or missing, they have found a documentation gap at the exact moment it costs them something — which is the only moment anyone is genuinely motivated to fix it. And because the agent can also write documentation, fixing it is the next sentence rather than a separate task for later.

Field note · the loop ran, but differently from the four steps above

The loop that worked is smaller and faster than the four steps above. When the agent said “no switch is recorded for that room” and the technician knew the answer, they said it in the next sentence and the agent wrote it down — one fact, one commit, in their name, with an undo. Nineteen such corrections landed in five weeks: which switch serves which building, a room with no access point of its own, a name people actually use. All but two came from one technician.

The four-step version — notice a gap, record it, write the article — barely ran. One gap was formally recorded, by the owner. Two new articles were written, by the owner, from tickets. Writing an article is a different act from stating a fact: it takes ten minutes nobody mid-ticket has. Design the loop for facts first. Articles follow when someone with time reads the facts.

One assumption was also withdrawn in practice: “proposed as a reviewed change” became “saved directly, reviewable afterwards, undoable by anyone on the team.” A review step before a one-line fact was friction nobody wanted; the undo tool and the change log turned out to be the review.

Two things follow, and they are the strongest arguments for the whole approach.

Documentation becomes a by-product of support work rather than a project that competes with it. Nobody has to schedule a documentation sprint that gets cancelled when something breaks.

The gaps get prioritised by real demand. The articles that get written are the ones technicians actually needed while solving actual tickets — which is a far better signal than any roadmap drawn up in advance about what documentation would be valuable.

Solving a ticket and improving the documentation stop being two competing activities.

Held · for one person. Open for the team.

For the technician who used it daily, the pull quote is simply true: the room map got filled in as a side effect of network tickets. For the rest of the team it has not yet been tested, because they have not yet been in the loop often enough to meet a gap. The closing stage takes this up.

Stage eight · a suggested rollout

Earn trust in stages

A sensible deployment does not begin by handing every capability to every technician. The following shape lets a team build confidence in something that reads before it is trusted with something that writes.

Phase 1

Prove it privately

One person — whoever will own it — runs the full set against real work. Every capability is on the table, because the aim is to find what is genuinely useful and what merely sounds useful before anyone else's time is spent.

Aim: a defensible answer to “which of these actually helps?”
As it happened

Three months, one user, the full set. Nine of twenty-two skills did not survive it.

Phase 2

One technician, reading only

A single experienced technician gets it on their real working machine, with a deliberately narrow set: questions about the estate, questions about tickets. Almost nothing that changes anything.

Restraint here is a feature. The first impression you want is “this knows things I would have had to go and look up” — and that impression is available entirely from read-only capability, at essentially no risk.

Aim: the assistant proves it holds more context than a person can.
As it happened · skipped, deliberately

The team got the tool together, and with the ability to reply on their own tickets, change a ticket's status and correct the knowledge base from the first day. The reasoning: each of those acts in the person's own name on things they already had authority over, every reply is shown before it is posted, and a read-only assistant would have had nothing to offer the two technicians with small queues. Five weeks on, no write has needed undoing. The paper's caution was right about automatic writes (Phase 5) and over-cautious about asked-for ones.

Phase 3

Iterate on real feedback

Fix what that technician found awkward, add the capabilities they asked for, and remove whatever went unused. This is where a deliberate handful of write capabilities is introduced — the ones that act as the person, on things they already have authority over: responding on their own tickets, publishing a correction to the documentation.

Aim: a set shaped by one real user rather than by anticipation.
As it happened

This phase never really finished. A hundred and two changes to the procedures in five weeks, most of them small, several prompted by one regular user: links that wrapped, a total that disagreed with the ticket system, a name the directory did not know. The regular user shaped the set; the occasional users mostly inherited it.

Phase 4

Roll out to the team

The whole team receives the set that survived Phase 3, pushed the same way as the pilot. The first user becomes the internal advocate, which is worth considerably more than an announcement.

Aim: everyday use, and the improvement loop running across the team.
As it happened · half of the aim

Everyday use: one of three. The loop running across the team: not yet. The advocate exists and is the single most important asset the rollout has. The next move, recorded in the team's own planning notes, is to let that person show their saved searches to the others rather than for the owner to announce anything.

Phase 5

Move from asked-for to automatic

Only once a capability has proved itself on demand should it be made to happen on its own. A useful example: matching a new ticket to a relevant documentation article — first as something a technician asks for, and only later as something attached to every new ticket automatically.

Aim: the assistant contributes without being invoked — having earned it.
As it happened · running in shadow mode

An unattended account now runs a classifier against new tickets every few minutes during working hours, through the same launcher, policy, audit log and model list as a person's session — in dry-run, writing nothing to the ticket system until a switch only the administrator can flip is turned. Forty-nine model calls so far, all observed, none acted on. This is exactly the order the paper asked for, and the harness had to grow a headless mode to allow it.

Why this order, rather than a faster one

An answer a technician asked for and found unhelpful is ignored and costs nothing. The same weak answer, published automatically onto every ticket, is noise on the team's primary working surface — and noise is very difficult to withdraw once people have learned to skip past it. Reading first is not caution for its own sake; it is the cheapest way to find out whether the thing is any good.

Stage nine · where it goes

What the second year looks like

The early value is retrieval — the estate becomes answerable. What follows is broader, and mostly falls out of having context and procedures in one place.

DirectionWhat it looks like in practiceFive weeks in
Onboarding A new technician asks the agent the questions they would otherwise queue up for a colleague, and is productive in weeks rather than months. Their questions also surface the gaps that long-serving staff no longer notice. One of the three technicians joined the team weeks before the rollout. Their first sessions were the directory, the queue and a room check — the colleague questions. Too early to say more.
Consistency A procedure captured as a skill is performed the same way by everyone, including at 4pm on a Friday. Improvements to it apply to the whole team at once. Held, mechanically: the scripts rank and format the same way for all four accounts, and 102 improvements reached everyone unasked.
Scheduled work The same skills a technician invokes by hand can run unattended — classification, reporting, routine checks — so automation and human use share one definition rather than drifting apart. Started, in shadow mode. One definition, one launcher, one policy. The older scheduled reports are being moved onto it.
Institutional memory Reasoning that used to close with the ticket accumulates instead. Departures stop being knowledge losses. Nineteen facts and two articles that did not exist in August. A small start.
Wider capability Read-only access to more systems, then — deliberately, and later — the ability to change things, one capability at a time and always attributable to a person. Printers and the network got read access. Nothing gained the ability to change infrastructure, and nobody has asked for it.

None of this requires a large team or a research budget. It requires documentation that is worth reading, a way of getting it in front of people at the moment they need it, and enough discipline to let capability follow demonstrated value rather than lead it.

The aim is not an assistant that knows about IT. It is one that knows about your IT — and knows more of it each month than it did the month before.

Stage ten · new in this edition

What we would write differently

Three observations from the person who built and ran this, after five weeks of watching other people use it. They are opinions, formed from a team of four, and they are the part of this paper most likely to be marked up again.

Author's note · 1

The knowledge base compounds for champions, not for everyone

The knowledge-base-as-context idea has been the most useful part of this for me. It may still be insider knowledge for the wider team. I no longer think it is realistic to expect every member of a team to contribute to building up the context. What I have seen instead is contribution in pockets: one person who notices gaps, fills them as they go, and gets visibly better answers for it, while the rest of the team benefits from the filled-in map without ever touching it.

If that is the real shape of it, then the work is to identify the champions early and make their contribution easy and visible, rather than to design for universal participation and be disappointed. The nineteen facts in Stage seven came from one person. That is not a failure of the loop; it may be how the loop normally works.

Author's note · 2

A knowledge base people can browse is a different thing from a folder of files

Publishing the knowledge base as an ordinary website — searchable, with an “edit this page” link on every article, rendered from the same data the agent reads — did more to engage the team and explain the benefit than any description of the agent did. People could see what the agent knew. They could see that it was theirs, and that it was sometimes wrong, and that fixing it was a link away.

The original paper treated the context as “plain Markdown a person can read.” That is true and insufficient. A collection of Markdown files in a repository is readable by the people who already live in repositories. For everyone else, the human-facing rendering is part of the system, not a nicety, and a team deciding what to build should budget for it from the start.

Author's note · 3

I am still undecided about the terminal

For me the terminal is natural and productive, and I would not trade it. I think the one technician who uses the tool daily is coming around to it. I am not sure about the whole team, and the usage figures in Stage five-b are consistent with either reading: the two occasional users may be occasional because their queues are small, or because a black window with a blinking cursor is one more thing between them and the answer. Five weeks and four people cannot separate those.

What I can say is that the question has changed since August. The browser door now exists as a prototype, and the skills and knowledge base have become readable by assistant products a team may already own. The decision for non-technical staff is therefore no longer “build a web front end or not”; it is which front door to put in front of a context layer that is worth more than any of the doors. I would build the context layer first, and let the terminal keep the people it suits.

The concept held. The context and procedures mattered even more than the paper claimed; the tool that carries them mattered less.

This document describes a deployment concept and a suggested phasing, as written in August 2026, and marks it up against five weeks of use by one IT team through early October 2026. The figures in Stage five-b and in the field notes are that team's, drawn from its own audit logs, session records and version history, with people reduced to roles. It remains deliberately independent of any particular organisation, ticketing system, model provider, or endpoint management platform — the shape holds regardless of which you use, and so do most of the corrections.
