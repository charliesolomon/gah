A concept paper for IT teams

The agent that already knows your estate

Every IT team knows more than it has written down, and the parts it has written down drift out of date in ways nobody notices until they cost someone an afternoon. General AI assistance does not help much with this: it is fluent about technology in general and ignorant of yours.

This paper sets out one way to close that gap. It describes a concept — an assistant that carries your organisation's own documented knowledge and your team's own procedures — and an existing, working tool that implements it, which is open and intended to be adapted rather than adopted as-is. It covers the problem, the shape of the solution, how such a system is built and deployed, and a staged way to introduce it to a team without taking on much risk. The specifics of any one organisation are deliberately left out; the shape is what transfers.

Stage one · the problem

Your best documentation lives in three people's heads

Every IT team has the same asymmetry. The senior technician can tell you which switch serves that room, why the printer on the second floor is on a static lease, and which of two identical-looking sites uses the older firmware — instantly, from memory.

None of that is written down in a form anyone else can use quickly. Some of it is written down and wrong. Most of it was true when it was written and has drifted since.

The cost shows up in ordinary moments:

  • A newer technician spends forty minutes establishing something a colleague knows cold.
  • The one person who knows is on leave, at another site, or in a meeting.
  • A ticket gets solved correctly, and the reasoning evaporates the moment it closes.
  • Someone follows the documentation and it is out of date, so next time they don't bother.

That last one matters most. Documentation that has burned you once stops being consulted, which means it stops being corrected, which guarantees it keeps being wrong. Every team has a wiki in this condition.

Stage two · why not a general assistant

The value isn't the model. It's your context.

A general-purpose assistant is genuinely good at explaining a protocol or drafting a script. It knows nothing about your naming conventions, your site layout, your ticket queues, or the exception someone made three years ago that everything now depends on.

So the useful question is not “can a model help with IT work?” — it plainly can — but “how does a model come to know what our team knows?”

Generic assistant

“How should I troubleshoot an access point that's offline?”

Returns a competent, general procedure. Correct, and no faster than what the technician already knows.

Context-aware agent

“What do we know about the access points at the north site?”

Answers from your documented estate — models, firmware, addressing, the standing exceptions and why they exist.

The second question is the one technicians actually ask each other. It has never had a good answer that wasn't a person.

Stage three · what it is

Two things: your context, and your procedures

The implementation this paper draws on is GAH — the Good Agent Harness, an open project that provides the agent itself along with the machinery to deploy it to a team and govern what it may do. In use it is simply a terminal application a technician opens and talks to.

It is worth being precise about what GAH is and is not, because it is only half of what makes the result useful. GAH supplies the vehicle: the agent, the deployment mechanism, and the controls. It ships with no knowledge of your organisation and no opinion about how your team works — deliberately, since that is the part no external project could get right.

The other half is yours, and it is what the rest of this section describes: two bodies of material that you write, keep in your own version control, and improve over time.

Your organisational context

The documented facts of your estate: sites, networks, equipment inventories, conventions, people and their roles. Not pasted into a prompt each time — carried by the agent as a matter of course, so a technician can simply ask.

Your skills

A skill is a packaged procedure: how your team triages a ticket, drafts a reply, checks the status of a device, writes an article. Each declares what it is for and precisely which tools and data it may touch. Staff invoke them by name or just describe what they want.

Neither is exotic. What makes the combination valuable is that both are yours, both are versioned, and both improve without anyone reinstalling anything.

The tool is the vehicle. What your team knows is the cargo — and no two organisations carry the same load.

Stage four · the architecture

An open harness underneath, your policy on top

The design question for anything like this is where the boundary sits between what you build and what you inherit. Build too much and you maintain an agent framework forever. Build too little and you cannot make it safe for your environment.

GAH is itself built on an open agent harness (earendil-works/pi): the terminal interface, tool use, session handling. That harness is vendored into the project rather than merely depended upon. Upstream improvements can be pulled in deliberately, reviewed as a normal change, and pinned. On top of that sits a layer that is entirely yours.

Deployment policy
yours
Per-user configuration held centrally: which models may be used, which skills a given person receives, which credentials are present, and whether a capability is read-only or permitted to change something. Staff cannot self-modify what they have been granted.
Skills & context
yours
A shared set maintained centrally, refreshed on every launch so a correction reaches everyone without anyone acting — plus an optional personal set each technician can extend, which the refresh never overwrites.
Open agent harness
inherited · vendored
Terminal interface, tool execution, conversation and session management. Tracked upstream so improvements arrive, held at a reviewed version so they arrive on your schedule.

The separation is the point. You inherit the parts that are hard and generic, and own the parts that are specific and consequential — access, safety, and what your team is actually asked to do.

Safety is a property of the layer you own

Because the policy layer is yours, capabilities can be split rather than granted wholesale. Asking what a device's configuration is and changing that configuration are different permissions, even where they use the same underlying tool. A team can be given the first for months before anyone considers the second.

Equally, actions taken on your systems can be attributed to the person who took them rather than to a shared robot identity — which matters the first time you need to know who changed something.

What a skill actually looks like

A skill is a Markdown file with a short header. The header is the whole security model — everything below it is prose telling the agent how your team does the thing. 

---
name: ticket-triage
description: Analyze open support tickets and provide triage recommendations
allowed-tools: Bash(~/ops/bin/ticket-triage.sh:*), Bash(~/ops/bin/day-of-week.sh:*)
---

# ...instructions, examples, and rules follow in plain Markdown

name and description are how the agent decides the skill is relevant to what someone asked. allowed-tools is the hard boundary: this skill may run those two scripts and nothing else. It cannot read arbitrary files, call other systems, or reach for a tool nobody granted it.

Why the tools are wrapper scripts rather than raw commands

Every external system is reached through a small script rather than by giving the agent direct command access. That keeps credentials and connection details out of the skill definitions entirely — the skill knows a script exists, not how it authenticates — and it collapses permissions to one clear grant per skill instead of a sprawl of patterns.

It also makes the read/write split practical: where a script exposes named operations, a skill can be granted the ones that only read and denied the ones that change something, using the same underlying tool.

What the context covers

Ten Markdown documents, versioned like code and reviewed like code. Generically, they hold:

  • People and structure — staff and leadership, roles and titles, contact details, which site someone works from, who escalates to whom
  • Sites and networks — locations and their abbreviations, network topology, addressing
  • Equipment inventories — the managed estate, model by model, with the standing exceptions and the reasons for them
  • Working conventions — how the ticket system is actually used, priority definitions, tone and escalation expectations
  • Time-based facts — the recurring annual calendar, historical baselines to compare current activity against

This is the part that makes the difference between a competent general assistant and one that can answer “who owns this, where is it, and what did we decide about it last year?” It is also, deliberately, the least exotic part of the system: plain Markdown that a person can read, correct, and review.

Proven against real work

The library has been exercised against live ticket queues and live infrastructure — triaging real tickets, answering real questions about the estate, and running unattended on a schedule as well as interactively. The skills that survived are the ones that earned their place; several early ones were removed for being clever rather than useful.

The hard part was never the agent. It was writing down what the team already knew, in a form something else could use.

Stage six · getting it onto machines

Deployment is a push, not a project

Nothing about this requires visiting desks. The agent is delivered the same way you already deliver software to managed machines: a script pushed through your endpoint management platform, or a global policy applied to the relevant group.

A technician sees a shortcut appear. They click it, and they are in. There is no per-machine configuration to get wrong, no runtime for staff to install, and nothing they need to keep up to date — updates arrive when they next open it.

Removal is the same operation in reverse, which is worth saying plainly: piloting this with one person costs one push and can be undone with another.

One topology that works

The obvious next question is what actually lands on the endpoint. In the running implementation, the answer is: a shortcut, and nothing else. The agent itself runs in a session on a server the technician connects to.

Endpoint
Staff machine
  • A shortcut that opens a terminal session
No runtime. No credentials. No data at rest.
Server
Where the agent runs
  • The agent session itself
  • Skills and organisational context, checked out and refreshed at launch
  • The wrapper scripts that reach other systems — executed here
  • Credentials, held per user and readable only by that user
Elsewhere
Sources & systems
  • The model provider you choose
  • Version control holding skills and context
  • Ticketing system
  • Infrastructure and management APIs

endpoint → server over an ordinary remote session  ·  server → version control at launch  ·  server → systems via wrapper scripts  ·  server → model provider

Three consequences follow, and they are the reason for the shape:

  • Nothing sensitive is on the laptop. A lost or replaced machine is a hardware event, not a security one. No credential rotation, no data to wipe.
  • One place to update. Skills and context are refreshed when a session starts, so a correction reaches everyone the next time they open it — with no redeployment and nothing for staff to install.
  • Network access lives in one place. The server already has reach into internal systems, so endpoints need no new firewall exceptions or VPN paths just to make the agent useful.
  • The model is reached from your side. The server holds the provider credentials and makes the calls, so staff machines never hold those either — and the traffic leaves from infrastructure you control.

“So which model do we use?”

Whichever you decide. The agent is not tied to a particular model or vendor — the provider is a configuration choice, and a reversible one. Teams pick differently for good reasons: an existing cloud agreement, a procurement preference, where the data is permitted to go, or simply cost.

Two details make that choice manageable rather than merely possible:

  • It is set centrally, per person. Model access is part of the same per-user configuration that governs skills and credentials — not something staff select, and not something they can widen.
  • Models are named explicitly, never by wildcard. A provider adding or renaming a model cannot silently change what your team is using, or quietly move you onto something you have not evaluated or budgeted for.

Because the agent runs on your server, the provider is called from your infrastructure under your account. Changing provider later is a configuration change on one machine — not a redeployment, and nothing staff would notice beyond the answers.

The terminal is not the only front door

The topology settles two things independently: where the agent runs, and how people reach it. Everything above concerns the first. The second is a separate choice, and a terminal session is simply what this implementation happens to use.

A browser interface reaching the same server-side agent would change nothing architecturally — same skills, same context, same credentials, same controls, same single place to update — while removing the one thing that puts some people off before they have tried it. Not every technician is at home on a command line, and for a tool whose entire value depends on people choosing to use it, that is not a small consideration.

This has not been built. It is worth mentioning because it is nearer than it sounds: the underlying harness already supports running as a server that authenticated clients connect to over a defined protocol. A web front end would be another client of that server rather than a second system — and both could exist at once, for the same team, talking to the same agent. Someone who prefers the terminal keeps it; someone who does not, never sees it.

That is the more general point, and it applies well beyond the interface: the pieces here are deliberately separable. Where it runs, how people reach it, which model answers, and what any given person is permitted to do are four independent decisions, and none of them is load-bearing for the others.

This is one deployment, not a requirement

A server session happens to answer several problems at once, which is why it was chosen — but nothing in the concept depends on it. The agent could run locally on each machine, or in a container per user, and the trade-offs simply move: local execution removes a dependency and a network hop, at the cost of putting credentials and updates back onto endpoints.

The parts worth carrying across any topology are the principles rather than the boxes: keep credentials off endpoints, keep skills and context in version control, and refresh them at launch so nobody is running a stale copy.

Stage seven · the part that compounds

It improves because people use it

This is the difference between a tool that plateaus and one that appreciates, and it turns the documentation problem from Stage One inside out.

When a technician asks the agent about part of the estate and the answer is thin, wrong, or missing, they have found a documentation gap at the exact moment it costs them something — which is the only moment anyone is genuinely motivated to fix it. And because the agent can also write documentation, fixing it is the next sentence rather than a separate task for later.

Someone asks

A technician working a ticket asks about a site, a device, a convention.

The agent answers from your documentation

Fully, partly, or not at all — and it says which.

The gap is visible

A missing or stale answer is now a concrete, named gap rather than a vague sense that the wiki is bad.

They fix it from the agent

The correction is proposed as a normal reviewed change, in their name, without leaving what they were doing.

→ and every later question, by anyone, is answered from the better version

Two things follow, and they are the strongest arguments for the whole approach.

Documentation becomes a by-product of support work rather than a project that competes with it. Nobody has to schedule a documentation sprint that gets cancelled when something breaks.

The gaps get prioritised by real demand. The articles that get written are the ones technicians actually needed while solving actual tickets — which is a far better signal than any roadmap drawn up in advance about what documentation would be valuable.

Solving a ticket and improving the documentation stop being two competing activities.

Stage eight · a suggested rollout

Earn trust in stages

A sensible deployment does not begin by handing every capability to every technician. The following shape lets a team build confidence in something that reads before it is trusted with something that writes.

Phase 1

Prove it privately

One person — whoever will own it — runs the full set against real work. Every capability is on the table, because the aim is to find what is genuinely useful and what merely sounds useful before anyone else's time is spent.

Aim: a defensible answer to “which of these actually helps?”
Phase 2

One technician, reading only

A single experienced technician gets it on their real working machine, with a deliberately narrow set: questions about the estate, questions about tickets. Almost nothing that changes anything.

Restraint here is a feature. The first impression you want is “this knows things I would have had to go and look up” — and that impression is available entirely from read-only capability, at essentially no risk.

Aim: the assistant proves it holds more context than a person can.
Phase 3

Iterate on real feedback

Fix what that technician found awkward, add the capabilities they asked for, and remove whatever went unused. This is where a deliberate handful of write capabilities is introduced — the ones that act as the person, on things they already have authority over: responding on their own tickets, publishing a correction to the documentation.

Aim: a set shaped by one real user rather than by anticipation.
Phase 4

Roll out to the team

The whole team receives the set that survived Phase 3, pushed the same way as the pilot. The first user becomes the internal advocate, which is worth considerably more than an announcement.

Aim: everyday use, and the improvement loop running across the team.
Phase 5

Move from asked-for to automatic

Only once a capability has proved itself on demand should it be made to happen on its own. A useful example: matching a new ticket to a relevant documentation article — first as something a technician asks for, and only later as something attached to every new ticket automatically.

Aim: the assistant contributes without being invoked — having earned it.
Why this order, rather than a faster one

An answer a technician asked for and found unhelpful is ignored and costs nothing. The same weak answer, published automatically onto every ticket, is noise on the team's primary working surface — and noise is very difficult to withdraw once people have learned to skip past it. Reading first is not caution for its own sake; it is the cheapest way to find out whether the thing is any good.

Stage nine · where it goes

What the second year looks like

The early value is retrieval — the estate becomes answerable. What follows is broader, and mostly falls out of having context and procedures in one place.

DirectionWhat it looks like in practice
Onboarding A new technician asks the agent the questions they would otherwise queue up for a colleague, and is productive in weeks rather than months. Their questions also surface the gaps that long-serving staff no longer notice.
Consistency A procedure captured as a skill is performed the same way by everyone, including at 4pm on a Friday. Improvements to it apply to the whole team at once.
Scheduled work The same skills a technician invokes by hand can run unattended — classification, reporting, routine checks — so automation and human use share one definition rather than drifting apart.
Institutional memory Reasoning that used to close with the ticket accumulates instead. Departures stop being knowledge losses.
Wider capability Read-only access to more systems, then — deliberately, and later — the ability to change things, one capability at a time and always attributable to a person.

None of this requires a large team or a research budget. It requires documentation that is worth reading, a way of getting it in front of people at the moment they need it, and enough discipline to let capability follow demonstrated value rather than lead it.

The aim is not an assistant that knows about IT. It is one that knows about your IT — and knows more of it each month than it did the month before.

This document describes a deployment concept and a suggested phasing. It is deliberately independent of any particular organisation, ticketing system, or endpoint management platform — the shape holds regardless of which you use.
